Colosseum

Short history

The Colosseum, also known as the Flavian Amphitheatre, was commissioned by the emperors Vespasian and Titus as a public venue that showcased Rome’s architectural ambition and imperial identity. Completed in 80 CE, it hosted spectacles that ranged from gladiatorial contests to staged hunts and dramatic performances. Its tiered seating, vaulted corridors, and numbered entrances made it possible to manage large crowds efficiently—an early example of large-scale event design. The amphitheatre’s structure combined concrete, stone, and brick, with a complex system of arches distributing weight. Beneath the arena floor, the hypogeum contained tunnels, lifts, and holding cells that enabled rapid scene changes and animal releases. Over time, earthquakes, stone reuse, and weathering damaged the building, yet its outline remains one of the most recognizable silhouettes in the world. As a landmark, the Colosseum reflects both the grandeur and the contradictions of ancient entertainment. Today it serves as a focal point for preservation, tourism, and historical reflection.
